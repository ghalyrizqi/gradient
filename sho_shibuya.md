You're approaching this the right way.

Most people study **the output** (the gradients). You should study **the system** that creates the output.

After spending time looking at Sho Shibuya's body of work, I think there are roughly **7 rules** behind it.

---

# Rule 1 — The newspaper is not the subject

This is the first thing most people get wrong.

They think:

```text
Newspaper + Sunrise
```

But Sho is actually doing:

```text
World Events + Time
```

The newspaper is merely the container.

His work is really:

```text
A day happened.

The sun rose anyway.
```

That's the artwork.

---

# Rule 2 — The gradient is emotional, not realistic

Look carefully.

Many of his skies are not accurate representations of what a sunrise looked like.

Instead they feel like:

```text
Memory of a sunrise
```

rather than

```text
Photograph of a sunrise
```

This is crucial.

Don't pull exact colors from weather APIs.

Do:

```ts
weather
→ mood
→ palette
```

not

```ts
weather
→ palette
```

Example:

| Weather | Mood       | Palette           |
| ------- | ---------- | ----------------- |
| Clear   | Optimistic | Saturated blues   |
| Rain    | Quiet      | Gray lavender     |
| Fog     | Nostalgic  | Desaturated peach |
| Storm   | Heavy      | Deep crimson      |

---

# Rule 3 — Most gradients follow a structure

After examining many pieces, I noticed a recurring hierarchy.

Not exact colors.

Structure.

```text
Dark Cool
↓
Cool
↓
Neutral
↓
Warm
↓
Hot
↓
Dark Warm
```

Something like:

```text
Ultramarine

↓

Blue

↓

Lavender

↓

Pink

↓

Orange

↓

Crimson
```

The transitions matter more than the colors.

---

# Rule 4 — Horizon placement is never random

This one is huge.

Most AI images put the horizon in the center.

Sho almost never does that.

Typically:

```text
5% – 15%
from bottom
```

Example:

```text
┌─────────────┐
│             │
│             │
│             │
│             │
│             │
│             │
│             │
│             │
│-------------│
└─────────────┘
```

The sky dominates.

That's why it feels contemplative.

---

# Rule 5 — Imperfection is controlled

The gradients look smooth.

But zoom in.

You'll find:

```text
pigment

paper absorption

slight streaks

tiny tonal shifts
```

Never enough to notice consciously.

Just enough to feel human.

If you code this:

```ts
noiseOpacity = 0.02
```

not

```ts
noiseOpacity = 0.15
```

Almost invisible.

---

# Rule 6 — Repetition creates meaning

One painting:

```text
Nice gradient.
```

100 paintings:

```text
Meditation on time.
```

This is why your website should be archival.

I would store:

```text
2026/06/24

2026/06/25

2026/06/26
```

forever.

The collection becomes the artwork.

---

# Rule 7 — Restraint

Probably the most important lesson.

Sho's work constantly asks:

> What can I remove?

Not:

> What can I add?

Most generative artists add:

```text
Clouds

Birds

Stars

Mountains

Sun

Ocean

Trees
```

Sho removes.

Eventually you're left with:

```text
Color

Paper

Date
```

And somehow that's enough.

If I were building your project:

✅ Daily ritual

✅ Deterministic generation

✅ Archival structure

✅ Weather influence

✅ Horizon placement

✅ Minimalism

---

# What I would NOT borrow

❌ New York Times

❌ Exact gradients

❌ Exact proportions

❌ Exact concept

❌ Exact visual signature

Because then you're making a clone.

# What I think your version could become

You live in Indonesia.

Imagine this instead:

```text
Every day at sunrise

Bandung weather

Bandung humidity

Bandung air quality

Bandung season

↓

Generated painting
```

The masthead isn't a newspaper.

It's your own publication.

Maybe:

```text
THE DAILY SKY

or

TODAY ABOVE JAVA

or

GHALY'S WEATHER REPORT

or

ARCHIVE OF MORNINGS
```

Then after 3 years you'll have:

```text
1095 paintings
```

all generated by the same rules.

At that point people won't compare it to Sho anymore.

They'll recognize it as its own visual system.

That's when the project becomes interesting. The goal isn't to reproduce his paintings. The goal is to reverse-engineer the principles—ritual, time, restraint, atmosphere—and build a machine that speaks with your own voice.
